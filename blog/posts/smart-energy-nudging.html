Research in progress · IVCCE, Plaksha University

Can energy data become useful at the right moment?

I am building a WhatsApp energy coach around smart-socket readings. The research question is whether a clear explanation or optional suggestion can help people understand their appliance use. A message is not evidence of energy saved.

A smart socket can measure electricity use. A chart can show a pattern. But neither automatically answers the questions a person might have: what is this appliance doing, is this reading reliable, and is there anything I want to change?

That gap is why I joined this work at the Indorama Ventures Center for Clean Energy (IVCCE) at Plaksha University. The project combines software, energy data and behaviour research. I care about the technical system, but the real question is human: can information arrive in a form that helps someone make sense of it without taking control away from them?

I have learned to keep two possible outcomes separate. One is whether people understand and use the coach. The other is whether measured energy use changes. Engagement does not prove savings, and an energy difference would need careful analysis before we could explain why it happened.

Designing the path from reading to response

The system receives timestamped readings from a smart socket, checks the record for coverage and reliability, and calculates usage before preparing an explanation or an optional suggestion. Each step can fail in a different way. A device may be mapped incorrectly. Data may be missing. A pattern may look unusual without showing why it occurred.

01 / MEASURERead the socketTimestamped power readings from a selected appliance.
02 / CHECKTest reliabilityConfirm access, device mapping and data coverage.
03 / EXPLAINUse plain languageAnswer a question or offer an optional suggestion.
04 / EVALUATESeparate outcomesStudy understanding and energy use independently.

Give each part a job it can do well

Early versions gave an AI model too much responsibility. It could interpret the data and shape an answer, which made the system feel flexible but also made the important decisions harder to reproduce. Energy calculations, access permissions and research eligibility should not depend on a model producing a plausible sentence.

So I moved those responsibilities into deterministic code. It checks which device a person is allowed to access, validates the readings, calculates energy in kilowatt-hours and decides whether a reminder is eligible. Models can help with language, such as interpreting an open-ended question or preparing a transcription. They do not grant access, calculate energy or decide that a household should receive a nudge.

Measured and checkedCode owns the facts

Authorised device scope, data quality checks, energy totals and eligibility rules. No guessed electricity cost when a verified tariff is not available.

Flexible but boundedModels help with language

Open questions and voice transcription can use language tools, behind the same access and calculation checks.

This division also helps when something goes wrong. If a daily total looks strange, we can inspect the readings and calculation. If an answer sounds wrong, we can review the language step without changing how energy was computed.

WhatsApp is a choice of setting, not proof of impact

The first prototype used Discord. It handled text, model replies and voice commands, but it did not match the setting we wanted to study. We moved the interaction to WhatsApp to test the coach in a more familiar kind of conversation. That is a design choice, not evidence that households prefer it. People can ask about current or past use, request a chart, send a voice note, set reminder preferences or choose quiet hours. They can pause, opt out or withdraw from research.

Those options matter because a helpful prompt for one person may feel intrusive to another. A reminder should be optional and controlled by the person receiving it. The system should also avoid turning a pattern into a diagnosis. A reading alone cannot prove that an appliance is broken or wasting energy.

Privacy shapes what the system keeps

Collecting every conversation might seem useful for improving a coach, but it would also retain details the research does not need. The current design separates permission to operate the coach, permission to analyse research outcomes and any future permission to improve shared language resources.

KEEPStudy essentialsConsent state, preferences, delivery labels and defined outcomes.
EXCLUDEPrivate conversationsRaw message text, voice audio, names, phone numbers and credentials.
SEPARATEPersonal from sharedFuture learning examples need consent, redaction and human review.
RESPECTPause or leavePeople can change preferences or withdraw from the study.

I want those boundaries to be part of how the product works, not promises added after the system is built. The same is true of data quality: missing periods should be visible, not quietly filled in to make a chart look complete.

Software checks are not household evidence

The system has been exercised through automated tests and local dry-runs for calculations, permissions, preferences, charts, voice handling and message paths. Those checks help find software defects. They do not tell me whether households will use the coach, whether messages feel timely, or whether anyone changes an energy habit.

Our planned Mumbai installation targeted smart sockets on ten geysers. The installation attempt did not succeed. No participant, engagement or energy result came from it. That was a difficult but useful boundary: a technically functioning bot is not the same as research working in someone's home.

Some household-facing details still need careful handling too. Earlier aggregate records were incomplete, so daily and monthly answers now need to be reconstructed from reliable minute-level readings. If a day does not meet the data-quality threshold, the system should say so rather than inventing a complete picture. The same rule applies to cost: without a verified tariff, the coach should not turn usage into a made-up bill estimate.

The next steps are gates, not a launch date

FIRSTInstall and calibrateConfirm hardware, appliance mapping, time settings and reliable readings.
THENEstablish a baselineCollect consented data and exclude incomplete periods.
AFTER REVIEWConsider a small studyAgree on intervention rules, supervision and opt-out before starting.

The current roadmap proposes 21 reliable baseline days, followed by a 30-day silent observation period in which possible anomalies are reviewed without sending automated messages. A limited opt-out-capable intervention could follow only if installation, false-positive checks and supervision are acceptable. These are planning stages, not an approved or completed study protocol.

Where I am today: the software and research method are clearer. The project has not yet shown household adoption, energy savings or causal impact.

I keep returning to the difference between a useful signal and a useful intervention. A socket gives us a measurement. It does not give us permission, context or a reason to interrupt someone. The research has to connect those pieces carefully, and be honest when the evidence is not ready.
