A product story · outreach

The next person should not have to start over.

For one college event, two or three of us were coordinating around 600 contacts. The spreadsheet held the list. It could not reliably hold the whole conversation.

Open the live workspace ↗

Excel was a sensible place to begin. We could collect names, sort colleges, prepare messages and share a working list. The trouble started after someone made contact. Calls happened on personal phones, messages sat in different places, and context moved between teammates in quick conversations.

A row marked “contacted” left too much unanswered. Who spoke with the person? What did they ask for? Was there a registration link to send? Should someone call next week, or leave them alone? When the owner changed, the next teammate could inherit a name without inheriting the conversation.

I first thought we needed a more organised spreadsheet. Then I realised the real problem was the handoff. The next person needed enough context to continue with care, without asking the contact to explain everything again.

Make the next action visible

That became the centre of Outreach Brain: import the list a team already has, assign responsibility, record what happened, and make the next step explicit. A contact should not sit in a vague “done” state when the real work is still unfolding.

The first version supports Excel import, checks for duplicate and incomplete records, and connects contacts with events and registrations. A teammate can record a call or message outcome, keep a note, assign a follow-up and set a reminder. The point is not to make every interaction elaborate. It is to give a team a shared memory for the details that change what should happen next.

That shared record matters most when work crosses people. One person may make the first call, another may answer a question about an event, and someone else may need to confirm a registration. If each person sees the relevant history and their own next task, the team has a better chance of continuing the relationship instead of restarting it.

Different people need different views

As the tool took shape, I had to think about who should see and change what. An admin needs to manage the workspace and team access. Members need to work with contacts and follow-ups. Someone checking overall progress may need a read-only dashboard, without access to every person's contact details.

That is why permissions and exports belong in the product from the start. A team should be able to limit access by role and still take its records elsewhere. Import matters because people already have work in spreadsheets. Export matters because they should remain in control of it.

Keep the useful parts of a spreadsheet

I do not think every team needs to move every list into a new system. For one person or a small, short-lived list, Excel may be the clearest tool. It is flexible, familiar and easy to inspect. A shared workspace becomes more useful when several people need the same history, ownership and follow-up rhythm.

Import and export let those tools work together. That decision also keeps the product honest about its job: coordinate the conversations that need coordination, rather than replacing a format people already know how to use.

Two outreach teams tried versions of the tool, and I asked people with user-interface experience to challenge the workflow. Their feedback made me reconsider what belonged on screen. Every extra status, field or button asks a teammate to learn and maintain one more thing. I keep coming back to a simple question: does this detail help someone make a better next move?

What still needs care

The current WhatsApp connection uses an unofficial bridge intended for controlled testing. Delivery labels are provider-reported signals, not proof that a person read or acted on a message. Message sending has its own permissions. Those boundaries matter before a team relies on the tool more widely.

The live application presents itself as Nudgen. I still think of the problem as Outreach Brain: how can a small team handle a large contact list without turning people into rows or losing the thread between conversations?

I built the first version to solve a problem I faced. The more useful test now is whether another team can use it without me standing beside them to explain every step. I want to learn where it saves effort, where it adds friction, and which parts should stay in a spreadsheet.
