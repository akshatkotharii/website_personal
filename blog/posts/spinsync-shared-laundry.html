SpinSync · a shared-laundry project

A free machine should not be a guessing game.

We kept making the same trip to the hostel laundry room, only to find every machine occupied. SpinSync began as an attempt to make that small daily system easier to see, share and trust.

A useful status view should help someone decide whether to go, wait or join the next turn.

SpinSync took shape during a two-semester Innovation Lab and Grand Challenge project at Plaksha University in Mohali. Our six-person team was asked to work on a real problem. I led solution design, and the first answer seemed obvious: build an app where students could book a washing machine.

But a booking only tells us what someone planned to do. It does not tell the next student whether a machine is actually running, whether a cycle has finished, or whether anyone has collected the clothes. We needed to understand the room before deciding what software belonged in it.

Start with the routine, not the feature list

We surveyed residents from 16 to 21 April 2026 and received 70 submissions. Students described several linked frustrations: finding a machine already occupied, walking over just to check, waiting for clothes to be removed, and trying to work out whose turn came next.

80%56 of 70 said clothes were left in machines after cycles.
73%51 of 70 found machines occupied when they wanted one.
87%61 of 70 physically checked whether a machine was free.
46of 70 reported waits of 30 minutes or more in the named survey categories.

These answers helped us locate the friction. They do not prove that a booking app, reminder or sensor will solve it. The sample also has a clear limit: 62 of the 70 responses came from H2 boys hostel. We should not present it as a campus-wide estimate. A fresh study needs more balanced participation and consistent information about hostel and machine access.

That caveat changed how I thought about “validation.” The survey was useful because it gave us a better next question, not because it approved every feature we had imagined.

A booking app grew into a shared service

We began sketching a system around the whole laundry cycle: see a machine’s status, reserve a turn or join a queue, receive a reminder when a cycle is near its end, and report a fault. An admin view could help someone review machine issues and exceptions. The aim is to make the next step visible to the people sharing the room.

We also explored smart sockets as a way to estimate when a machine starts or stops drawing power. That signal could help update a status, but it cannot tell whether clothes have been removed. Different cycles and faults can also produce patterns that are hard to interpret. Any pilot needs to compare socket readings with what is actually happening at the machine, and provide a manual fallback for hardware or network problems.

Fairness needs human judgement

The difficult cases are not edge cases for the people involved. Someone may be delayed for a genuine reason. A machine may fail. A sensor may report the wrong state. If the system turns those uncertainties straight into penalties, it could make the room feel less fair instead of more organised.

Design principle: use clear reminders, reasonable pickup buffers, a way to report problems, and human review for disputed events. Let the system make coordination easier without pretending it knows more than its evidence supports.

This is why we treated accountability as a design question, not a punishment feature. The software should help people see what happened and resolve a problem, while leaving room for context that a machine cannot observe.

What a small pilot should tell us

The course project is complete, and I am continuing SpinSync independently. Before building a larger system, I want to refresh the survey, speak with hostel residents and decision-makers, and validate the smart-socket signal on a limited number of machines.

A controlled pilot should give us evidence about practical questions: do people check availability before walking over? Are machine states accurate enough to rely on? Do reminders help clothes get collected sooner? Do queues feel understandable and fair? What happens when a machine or connection fails? We would need to agree on those measures before deciding whether the system deserves to grow.

There is no public demo yet. SpinSync has not been shown to reduce waiting, disputes or pickup delays in a live hostel. That is the point of the next step: find out what works in the room, not just what looks convincing in a prototype.

I still like the original idea because it is ordinary. Shared laundry is a small system, but it brings together people, machines, timing and trust. Designing for the messy moments between those parts has been more useful than starting with an app screen and assuming the rest will take care of itself.
