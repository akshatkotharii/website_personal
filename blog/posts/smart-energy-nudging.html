Research in progress · IVCCE, Plaksha University

Can energy data become useful at the right moment?

I am working on a WhatsApp energy coach that turns smart-socket readings into clear explanations and optional, timely suggestions. The research is about whether that helps people understand and act—not about assuming that a message saves energy.

This project matters to me because energy use is part of everyday life, but the information behind it is often hard to act on. A smart socket can measure electricity use. A chart can show a pattern. Neither, by itself, tells someone what the pattern means or what they might want to do next.

At the Indorama Ventures Center for Clean Energy (IVCCE) at Plaksha University, I have been building a privacy-conscious system around that gap. The research question is deliberately two-part: can people understand appliance data more easily, and what happens to measured energy use? Those are different outcomes. We need to measure both.

The idea, in four steps

01 / MEASURERead the socketTimestamped power readings from an appliance.
02 / CHECKCheck the dataConfirm access, coverage and reliability.
03 / EXPLAINUse plain languageAnswer a question or offer an optional suggestion.
04 / EVALUATELook for evidenceStudy engagement and energy outcomes separately.

How the project changed as I tested it

My first version gave an AI model a broad role: understand a question, interpret the data and suggest an answer. Testing made me uncomfortable with that design. Energy totals, user permissions and study claims need to be repeatable. A fluent answer is not useful if its calculation or source is wrong.

So I narrowed the model’s job. Deterministic code now handles who can access which device, checks the readings, calculates energy use and decides whether a reminder is eligible. Local language models help with open-ended wording; local speech recognition turns a voice note into text. The model does not decide permissions, calculate energy, or decide whether a household should receive a nudge.

Measured and checkedCode owns the facts

It scopes readings to an authorised device, checks data quality and computes energy in kWh. It does not guess a household’s electricity cost without a verified tariff.

Flexible but boundedModels help with language

Local models can help interpret open questions or transcribe voice. They sit behind the same permission and calculation checks.

WhatsApp is the interaction layer because it can support questions, charts, voice notes, reminders and feedback without asking someone to keep opening a separate dashboard. That may make the system easier to approach; it does not guarantee that anyone will engage with it.

A participant can ask about current or past appliance use, request a chart, adjust reminder timing, set quiet hours, pause, opt out or withdraw consent. Feedback such as “not useful” or “less often” should change how the system responds. A suggestion is optional, and the person remains in control.

Privacy is part of the research method

To study behaviour, it can be tempting to collect every conversation. I do not want the project to work that way. The current design separates permission to operate the coach, permission to analyse research outcomes, and any future permission to improve shared language resources.

KEEPWhat is neededPreferences, consent state, delivery labels, data quality and defined study outcomes.
LEAVE OUTPrivate conversationRaw messages, voice audio, names, phone numbers, credentials and private socket identifiers.
SEPARATEPersonal from sharedAny future learning examples would need consent, redaction and human review.
ALLOWPause or leavePeople can change preferences, pause reminders or withdraw from the research.
Why not train on every chat?

Raw conversations can include private details, mistaken assumptions and feedback that has not been reviewed. The project’s current direction is to begin with fixed, reviewed examples and only consider shared learning from consented, redacted, quality-checked material.

What has been tested—and what has not

The software has been checked through automated tests and local dry-runs: data calculations, access boundaries, preferences, charts, voice handling and message paths. A successful check shows that a defined software behaviour worked under its test conditions. It does not show that a household adopted the coach or used less energy.

Our planned Mumbai installation on ten geysers did not succeed. No household research, engagement result or energy result came from that attempt. I had to separate “the software works in a controlled check” from “the research has worked in people’s homes.” That distinction changed how I describe the project.

The next milestone is field evidence

FIRSTInstall and calibrateConfirm hardware, appliance identity, time settings and reliable readings.
THENCollect a baselineUse consented data and exclude incomplete periods instead of filling gaps.
AFTER REVIEWRun a limited studyBegin only when the intervention, supervision and opt-out path are ready.

The current roadmap includes a period of reliable personal baseline data and a silent observation stage before any automated anomaly message. Those are planning gates, not a final approved study protocol. A restart still needs successful installation, consent, calibration and a protocol agreed in advance.

Where I am honest today: the project has a working technical foundation and a clearer research method. It has not yet shown adoption, energy savings, or causal impact in homes.

I like this project because it keeps pulling me between software and real life. A socket reading is only a signal. A useful system also needs consent, good explanations, careful timing, reliable evidence, and a person who can say “not now.” I want the eventual research to be judged by what households experience and what the data can actually support.
